The short version

Your brain is still being built during your teen years. Other people are part of what builds it. That is why isolation is a risk and connection is protection (Blakemore & Mills, 2014; Orben et al., 2020).

Risk factor and protective factor

A risk factor makes a problem more likely. A protective factor makes it less likely, or makes it hit less hard (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2019).

Risk factor

Social isolation and loneliness

Social isolation means having few relationships and little contact with other people. Loneliness is the painful feeling of being alone or cut off, even when people are near (Office of the Surgeon General, 2023).

Protective factor

Social connection

Supportive relationships with family, friends and trusted adults, and feeling that you belong at school (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2019; Office of the Surgeon General, 2021).

Your brain is still being built

Your brain does not finish growing when you become a teenager. It keeps developing through your teens and into your twenties (Blakemore & Mills, 2014).

One of the parts still changing is the network your brain uses to understand other people: what they think, what they feel and what they mean. Scientists think the teen years may be a sensitive period for social learning, a time when the brain is especially ready to learn from other people (Blakemore & Mills, 2014).

During these years the brain also tidies itself up. Connections between brain cells that get used are made stronger. Connections that do not get used are trimmed away (Blakemore & Mills, 2014).

Why friends matter so much right now

In your teens you start to need time with people your own age more than you did as a kid. That pull toward friends is a normal part of growing up. It is not a distraction from development; it is part of it (Orben et al., 2020; Blakemore & Mills, 2014).

What isolation does

Here are four findings. Each one says what kind of study it was, because that changes how much it can tell us about teens.

  • Study of adults

    Researchers kept 40 adults alone for 10 hours, then scanned their brains. Pictures of people socializing produced a response in a craving-related area of the midbrain, similar to the one that pictures of food produce in hungry people (Tomova et al., 2020).

  • Small brain-scan study

    Adults played a simple computer ball-tossing game in a brain scanner. When the other players left them out, a brain area that also responds to the distress of physical pain became more active. It was a small study, so it is a clue, not a final answer (Eisenberger et al., 2003).

  • Animal study

    In an animal study, young mice were kept alone for two weeks right after they stopped nursing. They grew thinner myelin in the prefrontal cortex (the front part of the brain). Myelin is a fatty coating around the long wires of brain cells that helps signals travel quickly. Their thinner myelin did not recover when they went back to the group. The same two weeks alone later in life did not have this effect (Makinodan et al., 2012).

  • Review of animal studies

    Researchers who looked across many animal studies concluded that isolation during adolescence affects the brain and behavior in ways it does not at other ages (Orben et al., 2020).

What it means for mental health

A review of 63 studies, with about 51,000 children and teens in total, found that loneliness was linked to symptoms of depression and anxiety later on, up to nine years later. How long the loneliness lasted mattered more than how strong it felt (Loades et al., 2020).

Surveys of about one million 15- and 16-year-olds found that loneliness at school went up in 36 of 37 countries between 2012 and 2018 (Twenge et al., 2021).

People aged 15 to 24 now spend about 70% less time with friends in person than people their age did in 2003 (Office of the Surgeon General, 2023).

Connection matters for the body too. In adults, across 148 studies, people with strong relationships were about 50% more likely to still be alive when researchers checked back years later (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010).

What we do not know

  • Much of the brain evidence comes from animals or from adults, not from teens.
  • Studies of teens mostly show that loneliness and later problems go together. That is a link, not proof that one causes the other.
  • Choosing some time alone to rest or recharge is not the same thing as being isolated or feeling lonely.

What you can do

You do not need a big gesture. Small, regular contact counts: a text, a lunch together, a real conversation.
